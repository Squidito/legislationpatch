What Is the Senate Filibuster? How It Works in 2026

By James Shearn, EditorPublished May 2026Updated September 2026

A filibuster is not a single procedure with a rule of its own. The Senate defines it as "a loosely defined term for action designed to prolong debate and delay or prevent a vote on a bill, resolution, amendment, or other debatable question." The Congressional Research Service puts it the same way: filibustering "includes any use of dilatory or obstructive tactics to block a measure by preventing it from coming to a vote."

It is possible because Senate rules "establish no generally applicable limits on the length of debate, nor any motions by which a majority could vote to bring a debate to an end, or even limit it." There is one exception, and it is the subject of the rest of this page: "The only Senate rule that permits the body, by vote, to bring consideration of a matter to an end is paragraph 2 of Rule XXII, known as the cloture rule."

One consequence is structural, and CRS sets out both halves of it: "The votes of only a majority of Senators present and voting are needed to pass a bill on the floor. It can, however, require the votes of 60 Senators to invoke cloture on the bill in order to overcome a filibuster and enable the Senate to reach that vote on final passage." So "a bill's supporters have good reason to write it in a way that will attract the support of at least three-fifths of all Senators." Another is that a single bill can face the obstacle twice: "Most bills, indeed, are potentially subject to at least two filibusters before the Senate votes on final passage: first, a filibuster on a motion to proceed to the bill's consideration and, second, after the Senate agrees to this motion, a filibuster on the bill itself."

How the Modern Filibuster Works

The familiar image is the marathon speech. The Senate Historical Office describes it that way: "The type of filibuster most familiar to Americans is the marathon speech by a small group of senators, or even a single senator." Real ones exist. "South Carolina's Strom Thurmond filibustered for 24 hours and 18 minutes against the Civil Rights Act of 1957." The Senate's current record holder is more recent: "The record for the longest individual speech goes to New Jersey's Cory Booker, who spoke for 25 hours, 5 minutes, against the policies of the Donald J. Trump administration in April, 2025."

Standing and speaking is not what the word usually describes now. CRS records that "filibustering includes any tactics aimed at blocking a measure by preventing it from coming to a vote," and that cloture "has increasingly been used to overcome filibusters being conducted not only by debate, but through various other delaying tactics."

Much of the effect lands before anything reaches the floor, through the practice of placing a hold. A hold "has developed informally as a way for Senators to interpose such an objection in advance and without having to do so in person on the floor" — and CRS gives the whole of what that means: "Fundamentally, however, when a Senator places the hold, he or she is implicitly registering his or her intention to object to any unanimous consent request for consideration of the measure." The leadership then makes a scheduling judgment: "In the face of a threatened filibuster, for example, the majority leader may decide not to call a bill up for floor consideration or may defer calling it up if there are other, equally important bills the Senate can consider and pass with less delay." Holds and objections are the everyday mechanics of unanimous consent.

That also means cloture filings are not a filibuster count. CRS is explicit: "It would be erroneous to assume the presence or absence of cloture attempts is a reliable guide to the presence or absence of filibusters."

Cloture: The Mechanism That Ends a Filibuster

Cloture arrived in 1917. Before that, per the Senate, "the Senate rules did not provide for a way to end debate and force a vote on a measure. That year, the Senate adopted a rule to allow a two-thirds majority to end a filibuster, a procedure known as 'cloture.'" The threshold for legislation has moved once since: "In 1975 the Senate reduced the number of votes required for cloture from two-thirds of senators voting to three-fifths of all senators duly chosen and sworn, or 60 of the 100-member Senate."

There is more than one cloture threshold, and the differences matter. CRS states all three together: "The majority required to invoke cloture for most business is three-fifths of the Senators duly chosen and sworn, or 60 votes if there are no vacancies in the Senate's membership. However, invoking cloture on a measure or motion to amend the Senate's standing rules requires the votes of two-thirds of the Senators present and voting, or 67 votes if all 100 Senators vote. Additionally, under precedents established on November 21, 2013, and April 6, 2017, invoking cloture on presidential nominations requires a vote of a majority of Senators present and voting, or 51 votes if all 100 Senators vote."

Note what the first of those is measured against. Three-fifths of senators "duly chosen and sworn" is three-fifths of the seats actually filled, not a share of whoever happens to be present. CRS states the arithmetic exactly: cloture is invoked by "a vote of three-fifths of all Senators (60 votes if there is no more than one vacancy)." Enough empty seats lower the number; a single empty seat does not.

The mechanics are in the rule itself. A cloture motion must be "signed by sixteen Senators." It then, in CRS's summary, "lies over until the second calendar day on which the Senate is in session. For example, if the motion is filed on Monday, it lies over until Wednesday, assuming the Senate is in session daily." The vote comes "one hour after the Senate meets on the following calendar day but one," in Rule XXII's own words. The cloture process is covered in full here.

Invoking cloture does not end consideration; it caps it. Rule XXII allows "no more than thirty hours of consideration" before the Senate must vote on final disposition, and that cap can be extended rather than cut short: "The thirty hours may be increased by the adoption of a motion, decided without debate, by a three-fifths affirmative vote of the Senators duly chosen and sworn."

A failed cloture vote does not settle anything either. CRS: "there is no limit on the number of times that the Senate can vote on motions to invoke cloture on a bill, amendment, or other matter it is considering," and "Should a cloture motion be defeated, the majority leader may enter a motion to reconsider the cloture vote."

A vote for cloture is not a vote for the bill. Cloture "is the only procedure by which the Senate can vote to set an end to a debate without also rejecting the bill, amendment, conference report, motion, or other matter it has been debating" — ending debate and deciding the question are two separate votes.

Where the 60-Vote Threshold Does Not Apply

Presidential nominations. Start with what did not happen. Rule XXII's text does get amended by the ordinary route: the 1975 threshold change was one, and the current Senate Manual carries a third paragraph of Rule XXII added "As amended by S. Res. 16, 113-1, Jan. 24, 2013." That paragraph has nothing to do with nominations — it governs "a cloture motion on a motion to proceed to a measure or matter" that is "signed by 16 Senators, including the Majority Leader, the Minority Leader, 7 additional Senators not affiliated with the majority, and 7 additional Senators not affiliated with the minority," and CRS describes its effect as making such a motion "eligible for a vote on the next session day (as opposed to the second day of session, as would otherwise be the case)." It is here only to show that amending the rule is a real route, and a demanding one: cloture on a measure to amend the standing rules itself requires "two-thirds of the Senators present and voting."

The nominations threshold did not come that way. In the Senate's own account, "The Senate adopted new precedents in the 2010s to allow a simple majority to end debate on nominations." CRS dates both. On November 21, 2013 "the Senate reinterpreted the cloture rule to lower the threshold for invoking cloture for all nominations except to the Supreme Court"; on April 6, 2017 the Senate "reinterpreted Rule XXII to allow a majority of Senators voting, a quorum being present, to invoke cloture on nominations to the U.S. Supreme Court." Each time a majority overturned a ruling of the chair rather than changing the rule's text: "Proceedings of this kind have been called 'the nuclear option' because they required actions arguably at variance with established principles underlying Senate procedure."

The precedents lowered the threshold; they did not remove the step. "Importantly, neither the 2013 nor the 2017 precedent removed the potential need to invoke cloture on a nomination to reach a vote. The process for invoking cloture on a nomination remains the same."

Budget reconciliation. Reconciliation bills are protected by statute, not by a Senate precedent. 2 U.S.C. 641(e)(2) provides that "Debate in the Senate on any reconciliation bill reported under subsection (b), and all amendments thereto and debatable motions and appeals in connection therewith, shall be limited to not more than 20 hours." A statutory debate cap makes cloture unnecessary, the same construction Congress used for a Congressional Review Act disapproval resolution, where 5 U.S.C. 802(d)(2) provides that "In the Senate, debate on the joint resolution, and on all debatable motions and appeals in connection therewith, shall be limited to not more than 10 hours." What can ride in a reconciliation bill is limited: 2 U.S.C. 644, "Extraneous matter in reconciliation legislation," makes a provision extraneous and strikes it on a sustained point of order if it "does not produce a change in outlays or revenues," and 2 U.S.C. 641(g) bars any reconciliation bill "that contains recommendations with respect to the old-age, survivors, and disability insurance program established under title II of the Social Security Act."

Trade agreement implementing bills. 19 U.S.C. 2191(g)(2): "Debate in the Senate on an implementing bill or approval resolution, and all debatable motions and appeals in connection therewith, shall be limited to not more than 20 hours."

War Powers measures. The War Powers Resolution sets deadlines rather than a debate cap. Under 50 U.S.C. 1546, a concurrent resolution introduced under section 1544(c) "shall be reported out by such committee together with its recommendations within fifteen calendar days," and once reported it "shall become the pending business of the House in question" and "shall be voted on within three calendar days thereafter, unless such House shall otherwise determine by yeas and nays." A separate provision, 50 U.S.C. 1546a, covers a different vehicle — "Any joint resolution or bill introduced in either House which requires the removal of United States Armed Forces engaged in hostilities" without a declaration of war or specific statutory authorization — and caps debate on a veto message for one of those at "twenty hours in the Senate."

Why This Shapes the Bills You Follow Here

The clearest effect is on what never gets a vote at all. CRS describes the calendar arithmetic: "there often are more bills that are ready to be considered on the Senate floor than there is time available for acting on them. Under these circumstances, the majority leader may be reluctant, especially toward the end of a Congress, even to call up a bill unless he can be assured that it will not be filibustered."

It also shapes bills that do move, before any vote is taken: "the prospect of a filibuster can persuade a bill's proponents to accept changes in the bill that they do not support but that are necessary to prevent an actual filibuster."

And when a measure travels one of the statutory paths above — a reconciliation bill, a CRA disapproval resolution, a trade implementing bill — the 60-vote question never comes up, because a statute has already capped the debate.

What Is a Continuing Resolution? Why the Government Runs on Temporary Funding

By James Shearn, EditorPublished May 2026Updated August 2026

A continuing resolution (CR) is a temporary appropriations law: it keeps the federal government funded at a rate the CR itself sets — in recent practice, based on the prior year's appropriations — while Congress finishes its regular annual spending bills. CRs are stop-gap measures, and they are routine: since the current fiscal calendar began with FY1977, Congress has enacted all of its regular appropriations bills before the start of the fiscal year only four times, most recently for FY1997.

What a CR Actually Does

The first thing a CR does is set a funding rate. In recent practice that rate has been based on the prior year's appropriations acts, prorated for the CR's duration. The pending FY2027 stop-gap works exactly this way: H.R. 9770 funds continuing projects and activities "at a rate for operations as provided in the applicable appropriations Acts for fiscal year 2026 and under the authority and conditions provided in such Acts."

A CR also says exactly when it ends — and not just with a date. Under H.R. 9770, funding for any given program runs until whichever comes first: a regular FY2027 appropriation for that program is enacted, a FY2027 appropriations act is enacted without any provision for it, or December 4, 2026 arrives. That three-way trigger is the mechanism that lets a stop-gap step aside program by program as real appropriations arrive.

CRs also carry what appropriators call "anomalies" — additional provisions that give specific programs additional funding, exceptions to the CR's terms and conditions, or other legislative changes, where continuing flatly at the prior-year rate would misfire.

What a CR Is Not: the Minibus

A CR is sometimes confused with a "minibus," but they are different instruments. A minibus is consolidated appropriations legislation containing only some of the regular appropriations bills — full-year funding in a smaller package, not temporary funding. Our omnibus vs. minibus explainer covers how Congress packages the regular bills; this page covers the stop-gap that bridges the gap until those packages pass.

What CRs Don't Allow

The flip side of continuing at last year's rate is that a CR freezes the shape of the government. H.R. 9770's own text bars new starts: no funding authority it grants "shall be used to initiate or resume any project or activity for which appropriations, funds, or other authority were not available during fiscal year 2026." Programs the prior year did not fund stay unfunded, and programs whose needs have grown stay at the prior-year rate unless an anomaly says otherwise. The longer a CR runs, the longer agencies operate under a budget written for a different year.

Why CRs Have Become the Default

Twelve regular appropriations bills are supposed to be enacted before October 1, when the federal fiscal year begins. Since FY1977, all of them have been in place on time only four times — most recently for FY1997. In every other fiscal year, at least some of the regular bills were not in place when the fiscal year began.

FY2026 shows the modern pattern end to end. The fiscal year opened under a continuing resolution — H.R. 5371, signed November 12, 2025, a package that also carried three of the twelve regular bills as full-year divisions. A three-bill minibus followed in January (H.R. 6938, Public Law 119-74), the five-division Consolidated Appropriations Act arrived February 3, 2026 (Public Law 119-75) — more than four months into the fiscal year it funds — and Homeland Security was finished last, on April 30, 2026 (H.R. 7147, Public Law 119-86). The omnibus vs. minibus explainer maps all twelve bills to the four laws they landed in.

Where Things Stand Now

FY2026 funding runs through September 30, 2026 — the Consolidated Appropriations Act makes appropriations "for the fiscal year ending September 30, 2026." The House passed the FY2027 stop-gap on July 21, 2026: H.R. 9770, 220-205 (Roll 272), which would continue funding at FY2026 rates from the start of the fiscal year until, at the latest, December 4, 2026. As of this update it awaits Senate action.

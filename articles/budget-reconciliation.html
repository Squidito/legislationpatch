What Is Budget Reconciliation? The Senate's 20-Hour Debate Clock

By James Shearn, EditorPublished May 2026Updated September 2026

Budget reconciliation is an expedited procedure for legislation that changes spending, revenue or the debt limit, made available under Section 310 of the Congressional Budget Act of 1974. The Congressional Research Service identifies what makes it different: "A distinctive feature of reconciliation is that debate time in the Senate is limited, which means cloture (and its requisite three-fifths majority vote) is not necessary to reach a vote on final passage." That is the whole mechanism — not a lower vote threshold, but a debate clock that runs out on its own, so there is nothing for a filibuster to prolong and no cloture vote to win.

Passage itself takes what ordinary passage takes. As CRS puts it, "The votes of only a majority of Senators present and voting are needed to pass a bill on the floor," and under Article I of the Constitution the Vice President "shall be President of the Senate, but shall have no Vote, unless they be equally divided."

It is used often and for large things. CRS records that reconciliation "has been used to reform entitlement programs (1996 and 2025), create new ones (2010), balance the federal budget (1997), and enact broad-based tax reform (2001, 2003, 2017, and 2025)."

How Reconciliation Works

It begins with the budget resolution. "Reconciliation directives (also known as reconciliation instructions) are a necessary step if Congress seeks to exercise the expedited procedures enumerated in the Budget Act," CRS writes, and there are three kinds: "to change laws providing for spending, to change laws providing for revenues, and to change the public debt limit." CRS notes one wrinkle: "A fourth instruction—to change the deficit—is interpreted as a combination of both revenue and spending instructions."

The statute sets out what happens next. Under 2 U.S.C. 641(b), where more than one committee is instructed, each "shall promptly make such determination and recommendations and submit such recommendations to the Committee on the Budget of its House, which, upon receiving all such recommendations, shall report to its House reconciliation legislation carrying out all such recommendations without any substantive revision."

On the Floor

The Senate debate limit is in the statute itself. 2 U.S.C. 641(e)(2) provides that "Debate in the Senate on any reconciliation bill reported under subsection (b), and all amendments thereto and debatable motions and appeals in connection therewith, shall be limited to not more than 20 hours." There is no House figure in that provision and no per-chamber split. Inside the 20 hours, CRS records two sub-limits: "Debate time on any first-degree amendment is limited to no more than two hours equally divided between the mover and the manager of the reconciliation bill. Debate on any second-degree amendment, debatable motion, or appeal is limited to one hour." They are sub-limits rather than additions: "Time spent in a quorum call during consideration of an amendment counts against this limit. All time spent in discussion on the floor during this period (on the reconciliation bill or on any amendment, motion, or appeal) counts toward the 20-hour debate limit. Time spent conducting votes does not." A separate clock governs resolving differences with the House: "In the Senate, Section 305(c) limits debate on a conference report to 10 hours, equally divided, and debate on any motion or appeal is limited to one hour."

The House does it differently: "In the House, consideration is typically structured by a special rule reported from the House Rules Committee, which sets the terms for debate and may permit specified amendments to be offered from the floor."

Amendments in the Senate are constrained too. "Amendments to reconciliation legislation must be germane," because "Section 310(e) of the Budget Act prohibits consideration in the Senate of non-germane amendments to reconciliation legislation, thereby limiting the ability of Senators to expand the scope of changes recommended by committees through amendments offered on the Senate floor."

Then comes the part everyone has seen a clip of. "Section 305 of the Budget Act ... specifies a limit on debate but not consideration," so "even after debate time has expired, Senators may continue to offer and decide additional amendments, but they cannot debate them." CRS calls the resulting stretch a "vote-arama": "During a vote-arama, the Senate typically uses unanimous consent agreements to make tranches of amendments in order and to set the parameters for their consideration—usually two minutes to identify and briefly summarize the amendment, evenly divided between sides, followed by a vote."

What Reconciliation Cannot Contain

The controlling section of the U.S. Code is headed "Extraneous matter in reconciliation legislation" (2 U.S.C. 644). CRS names it: "Section 313, also known as the Byrd rule," after Senator Robert C. Byrd, its principal sponsor. "The Byrd rule establishes six tests used to identify extraneous matter." A provision is extraneous in the Senate if:

  1. "It does not produce a change in outlays or revenues, including changes resulting from a change in the 'terms and conditions' under which outlays are made or revenues are collected (e.g., a change in the definition of qualified beneficiaries or a change in the definition of a person or entity subject to a tax).";
  2. "It produces an increase in outlays or a decrease in revenues when the instructed committee is not in compliance with its reconciliation directives.";
  3. "It is outside of the jurisdiction of the committee that submitted the title or provision for inclusion in the reconciliation measure.";
  4. "It produces a change in outlays or revenues that is merely incidental to the non-budgetary components of the provision.";
  5. "It would increase the deficit in any fiscal year beyond the years covered by the reconciliation measure."; or
  6. "It would amend Title II of the Social Security Act (the Old-Age, Survivors, and Disability Insurance program)".

The remedy is narrow rather than fatal to the bill: "A point of order made under the Byrd rule is surgical: If sustained, the offending matter is stricken from the text, but the rest of the measure remains before the Senate."

Waiving it is hard. "In the Senate, a motion to waive any of these points of order requires the affirmative vote of three-fifths of Senators, duly chosen and sworn (60 Members in a Senate with no more than one vacancy)."

The rule is younger than reconciliation. CRS records that "In 1985 and 1986, the Senate adopted the Byrd rule (named after its principal sponsor, Senator Robert C. Byrd) on a temporary basis as a means of curbing these practices," and that "In 1990, the Byrd rule was incorporated into the Congressional Budget Act of 1974 as Section 313 and made permanent (2 U.S.C. 644)."

Social Security Has Two Separate Protections

The first is not part of the Byrd rule at all. 2 U.S.C. 641(g) provides that "it shall not be in order in the Senate or the House of Representatives to consider any reconciliation bill or reconciliation resolution ... or any amendment thereto or conference report thereon, that contains recommendations with respect to the old-age, survivors, and disability insurance program established under title II of the Social Security Act." It binds both chambers and reaches amendments and conference reports as well as the bill, and CRS describes its effect as blunt: "This point of order, if raised and sustained, immediately ends consideration of a reconciliation bill by sending it back to committee."

The second is the sixth Byrd test, which reaches the same subject by cross-reference: under 2 U.S.C. 644(b)(1)(F), "a provision shall be considered extraneous if it violates section 641(g) of this title." That one applies in the Senate only, and strikes the provision rather than the bill.

The Senate Parliamentarian

"As the Senate's expert on the chamber's rules, precedents, and practices, the Senate Parliamentarian advises the presiding officer on the floor regarding the Senate's rules, including the Byrd rule." Most of the work happens before the floor: "In recent practice, reconciliation legislation to be considered in the Senate has received preemptive scrutiny by the Senate Parliamentarian in conjunction with Senate staff." CRS notes that "This tradition, known as a 'Byrd bath' or 'Byrd scrub,' allows for the identification of legislative text that would violate the Byrd rule in advance of its consideration on the floor."

The 2021 minimum-wage episode is in the record as a floor event, not a pre-floor ruling. CRS RL30862's table of actions taken under the rule lists, for the American Rescue Plan Act of 2021, a point of order against Sanders Amendment No. 972, "To provide for increases in the Federal minimum wage," on the ground that its budgetary changes were "merely incidental to non-budgetary components." The motion to waive was "Rejected, 42-58" on March 5, 2021, the point of order was "Sustained," and the amendment fell.

How Many Reconciliation Bills Per Budget Resolution

Not one. CRS states the limit precisely: "No more than one reconciliation bill may include provisions in response to each type of instruction in one budget resolution, for a maximum of three reconciliation bills." Because there are three types of directive — spending, revenue and the debt limit — three is the ceiling. What usually happens is the opposite of splitting: "In practice, Congress has generally combined the reconciliation submissions from every committee into a single, omnibus reconciliation measure."

What Has Passed Through Reconciliation

"Since 1980, Congress has considered 29 reconciliation bills and enacted 24" — four were vetoed and one did not pass the Senate.

Several of the best-known have no short title at all, because the Byrd rule took it. Of the FY2018 law, CRS writes: "The short title for this measure was struck by a point of order made pursuant to the Senate's Byrd rule. As a result, the official title of the measure as enacted is 'An Act to provide for reconciliation pursuant to titles II and V of the concurrent resolution on the budget for fiscal year 2018,' but the bill is sometimes referred to as the Tax Cut and Jobs Act." The same happened to P.L. 117-169, where "the official title of the measure as enacted is 'An Act to provide for reconciliation pursuant to title II of S.Con.Res. 14,' but the bill is sometimes referred to as the Inflation Reduction Act or Build Back Better Act." And to the most recent one: "The short title for this measure was struck by a point of order made pursuant to the Senate's Byrd rule prior to its adoption. As a result, the official title of the measure as enacted is 'An Act to provide for reconciliation pursuant to title II of H. Con. Res. 14,' but the bill is sometimes referred to as the One Big Beautiful Bill Act." The Congress.gov record dates its enactment to July 4, 2025.

One in that group kept its name. CRS lists the "Health Care and Education Reconciliation Act of 2010 (P.L. 111-152)" by its short title.

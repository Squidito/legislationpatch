How to Read a Federal Bill — A Practical Guide

By James Shearn, Editor Published May 2026Updated September 2026

A federal bill is built from a small number of recurring parts. This guide walks them in the order they appear — the enacting clause, the short title, findings and definitions, the section hierarchy, amendment language, effective dates and severability — and then covers how to find what changed in a long bill and how to read what members said about it.

Where to Find the Bill Text

Two places, for two different purposes: Congress.gov for a measure as it moves, GovInfo for the official published versions. What the statutes settle is which text is authoritative once a measure is law.

For an enacted measure the durable citation is the Statutes at Large. Under 1 U.S.C. 112, the Archivist of the United States "shall cause to be compiled, edited, indexed, and published, the United States Statutes at Large, which shall contain all the laws and concurrent resolutions enacted during each regular session of Congress", and the Statutes at Large "shall be legal evidence of laws". Under 1 U.S.C. 106a the original of a measure that becomes law is "received by the Archivist of the United States from the President"; where a veto is overridden, the same section routes it instead "from the President of the Senate, or Speaker of the House of Representatives in whichsoever House it shall last have been so approved". Either way the Archivist "shall carefully preserve the originals."

The Structure of a Bill

The enacting clause

Every Act of Congress opens with the same sentence, and the requirement is written in the U.S. Code. 1 U.S.C. 101 provides: "The enacting clause of all Acts of Congress shall be in the following form: 'Be it enacted by the Senate and House of Representatives of the United States of America in Congress assembled.'" The section's source note records where that wording was enacted — an ordinary act of Congress, "(July 30, 1947, ch. 388, 61 Stat. 634.)" So the form every Act opens with is prescribed by the U.S. Code, and that is where to look it up. A joint resolution uses a different opening prescribed by 1 U.S.C. 102: "Resolved by the Senate and House of Representatives of the United States of America in Congress assembled."

Before a House bill or joint resolution is introduced at all, its sponsor owes the Record an explanation of the constitutional basis for it. Clause 7(c)(1) of House Rule XII, which reaches those two forms and not simple or concurrent resolutions: "A bill or joint resolution may not be introduced unless the sponsor submits for printing in the Congressional Record a statement citing as specifically as practicable the power or powers granted to Congress in the Constitution to enact the bill or joint resolution."

The short title

The first numbered section usually names the Act. In H.R. 1011 of the 119th Congress it reads in full: "SECTION 1. SHORT TITLE. This Act may be cited as the 'Emergency Conservation Program Improvement Act of 2025'." That is what a short-title section does — it supplies the name by which the Act may be cited.

Findings and definitions

Many bills follow the short title with a findings section, a definitions section, or both. A findings section states what Congress is asserting as the factual basis for the measure: H.R. 1993 of the 119th Congress opens its second section "SEC. 2. FINDINGS. Congress finds the following:" and then numbers the findings.

A definitions section fixes the meaning of terms for that Act alone, and its terms govern the operative sections that follow. H.R. 165 of the 119th Congress: "SEC. 2. DEFINITIONS. In this Act: (1) Restricted fee status.--The term 'restricted fee status' means a status in which the Tribal land--".

The section hierarchy

The body of a bill is numbered sections (SEC. 1, SEC. 2), with subsections lettered (a), (b), (c), paragraphs numbered (1), (2), (3), subparagraphs lettered (A), (B), (C), and clauses in lower-case roman numerals (i), (ii), (iii). Longer bills group sections under TITLE headings. H.R. 1 of the 119th Congress runs the full depth: its SEC. 70102 carries a subsection "(a) In General.--", and elsewhere the same Act reaches subparagraph level with items such as "(E) Restrictions on internet expenses".

Amendment language

Most legislation changes existing law rather than writing on a blank page, and it does so with a small set of formulas. All four examples below are from H.R. 1 of the 119th Congress.

Striking and inserting replaces text in place: a provision "is amended by striking subsection (u) and inserting the following: '(u) Thrifty Food Plan.--' '(1) In general.--The term 'thrifty food plan' means the diet required to feed a family of 4 persons'"

Adding at the end appends new text to an existing provision: "is amended by adding at the end the following: '(E) Restrictions on internet expenses.--Any service fee'".

Amending to read as follows replaces a provision wholesale — everything after the colon is the new text, and the old text is gone. The formula in the bill is "is amended to read as follows".

Inserting after places new text at a named position: "is amended by inserting after the item relating to section 50923 the following: '50924. Space launch and reentry licensing and permitting user fees.'".

To see what any of these actually did, follow the citation the bill gives for the provision being amended. H.R. 1011 shows the pattern: "Section 401 of the Agricultural Credit Act of 1978 (16 U.S.C. 2201) is amended--". That parenthetical is the address in the U.S. Code of the law being changed. Why a sentence built almost entirely out of those addresses is hard to read is a separate subject.

Effective dates and severability

An effective-date provision says when a change starts to operate, and it is frequently not the date of enactment. H.R. 1: "Effective Date.--The amendments made by this section shall apply to taxable years beginning after December 31, 2025."

A severability clause says what survives if a court strikes part of the Act. H.R. 4371 of the 119th Congress carries one that runs to the actual mechanism: "Any provision of the this Act or an amendment made by this Act held to be invalid or unenforceable by its terms, or as applied to any person or circumstance, shall be construed so as to give it the maximum effect permitted by law, unless such holding shall be utterly invalid or unenforceable, in which event such provision shall be deemed severable from this Act and shall not affect the remainder of this Act". (The doubled "the this" is the bill's own typo, kept as printed.)

How to Find What Matters in a Long Bill

Three moves, each anchored in something the bill itself prints.

Read the section headings first. They are a map of the subjects the bill reaches, and in a long Act they are where to start.

Follow the amendment citations. A bill that amends existing law tells you exactly which provision it is changing; the change means little until you have read the provision being changed.

Look for "notwithstanding" clauses. The word signals that what follows operates despite other law. H.R. 1 of the 119th Congress carries one: "Notwithstanding section 508(l), the Board shall approve a policy or plan of insurance based on the pilot program under paragraph (1)--".

Congressional Record: What Members Said

The bill text is what Congress enacted; the Congressional Record is what members said about it. GovInfo describes it as "the official record of the proceedings and debates of the United States Congress", notes that "It is published daily when Congress is in session", and records that it "began publication in 1873, and is still published today."

CRS describes what the Record is and is not: "a substantially verbatim account of remarks made during the proceedings of the House and Senate, subject only to technical, grammatical, and typographical corrections." It "consists of four main sections: the proceedings of the House and Senate, the Extensions of Remarks, and the Daily Digest."

Two practical details from the same CRS guide. Page numbers carry a section letter: "The pages of the House proceedings are preceded by an 'H,' those of the Senate with an 'S,' those of the Extensions of Remarks section with an 'E,' and the Daily Digest with a 'D.'" And the Digest is the index: it "is the last section in each edition of the daily Record and serves as an index", through which "a reader can locate the times of meetings of both houses; measures reported, considered, or signed into law".

A Quick Reference: Bill Reading Checklist

  • Find the text on Congress.gov for status and history, or GovInfo for the official published version.
  • Skip the enacting clause — 1 U.S.C. 101 makes it identical in every Act.
  • Read the short title, then any findings and definitions sections before the operative text.
  • Scan the section headings, and the TITLE headings in a long bill, for the structure.
  • For each amendment instruction, follow the U.S. Code citation and read the provision being changed.
  • Check for "notwithstanding" clauses, effective dates and severability.
  • Look up the sponsor's constitutional authority statement and the floor debate in the Congressional Record, starting from the Daily Digest.

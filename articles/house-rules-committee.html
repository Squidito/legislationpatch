What Is the House Rules Committee? The Gatekeeper of Floor Action

By James Shearn, EditorPublished May 2026Updated September 2026

The House Rules Committee is the standing committee that writes the terms on which most major legislation is debated on the House floor. Clause 1(o) of House Rule X gives it jurisdiction over "Rules and joint rules (other than those relating to the Code of Official Conduct) and the order of business of the House" and over "(2) Recesses and final adjournments of Congress", and clause 5(a) of Rule XIII gives it "leave to report at any time" on "rules, joint rules, and the order of business."

What does a special rule actually do?

A special rule is a House resolution that sets the procedural terms for one or more named measures. The Congressional Research Service defines it as "a House resolution (H.Res.) that regulates floor consideration of one or more measures identified in its text", serving two purposes: "(1) to enable the House to consider specific measures by granting them privileged status, and (2) to establish the procedural framework for floor consideration of measures brought up under a rule."

A special rule does not take effect on its own. "Before privilege can be conferred on legislation via special rule, a majority of the House (with a quorum present) must first agree to the resolution", CRS notes — adding that special rules "almost always prevail, usually along party lines, largely because they are written in such a way as to ensure majority party support."

Most special rules do six things, per CRS: they identify "the procedural forum the House would use to consider a particular measure"; "allocate time for Members to engage in general debate"; "establish the base text open for debate and amendment"; "specify the nature of the amendment process (if any)"; waive the standing rules in whole or in part so points of order cannot be raised; and "provide for the motion to recommit on initial consideration of a bill or joint resolution."

Two of those deserve a closer look. A waiver is the rule's "ability to adjust the standing rules of the House in application to the specified measure" — for instance clause 4 of Rule XIII, which CRS describes as mandating "a 72-hour layover period for most committee reports before the reported measure would be eligible for floor consideration." CRS adds that "[i]n rare cases the language of the rule may include surgical waivers of specific standing rules", with a blanket waiver the more common form. A self-executing provision writes text into the bill at the moment the rule is adopted: when a rule provides that an amendment is considered as adopted, "it means that no separate vote would occur on the amendment(s)."

What are the four types of rules?

The Rules Committee names four standard types on its own website. "Rules are traditionally referred to along a spectrum, where on one end they are open and the other they are closed", it says. "While there is wide variation in the middle, there are certain standard kinds of rules."

Open rules "permit the offering of any amendment that otherwise complies with House rules, and allows debate under the 5-minute rule." CRS describes a fully open rule — one "that contains no amendment limitations beyond what the standing rules require" — as "rare in current practice".

Modified-open rules "operate much like an open rule, but have some restriction" on the universe of amendments, "either through a pre-printing requirement or an overall time limit on consideration of amendments." CRS gives the same test: a rule "with minimal restrictions on the ability to offer amendments but not fully open" — one requiring only, say, "that an amendment be printed in the Congressional Record in advance of consideration" — "would be referred to as modified open".

Structured rules "specify that only certain amendments may be considered and specify the time for debate." Under a structured rule the eligible amendments are the ones the Rules Committee prints in its report on the rule.

Closed rules "effectively eliminate the opportunity to consider amendments, other than those reported by the committee reporting the bill." In the CRS formulation, closed rules "prevent any Member from offering an amendment", while structured rules "restrict amendment opportunities in some way" without blocking them altogether.

Modified-open and structured are therefore not interchangeable labels. A modified-open rule restricts the universe of amendments by a pre-printing requirement or a time limit; a structured rule names the amendments that may be offered.

How long is debate under a rule?

One hour is the standard. "Most bills brought to the floor under a rule have a one-hour limit on general debate", CRS reports. That time goes to the managers — "in most cases, the chair and ranking minority member of the relevant committee(s) of jurisdiction" — "who would be expected to yield discrete portions of that time to Members on their side of the aisle seeking to engage in debate." Longer allocations are written into the rule when the measure warrants: H.Res. 260 in the 118th Congress "allocates seven hours and places that time under the control of the chair and ranking member of three of the five committees" — Energy and Commerce, Natural Resources, and Transportation and Infrastructure — with legislative jurisdiction over the bill.

Why does the House need special rules at all?

Because the House's default schedule is not where its business happens. "A daily order of business is defined in House Rule XIV," CRS notes, but "most measures reach the floor as privileged interruptions of the daily order." A special rule is how a measure gains that privilege on terms the House sets for it, and Rule XIII, clause 5(a)(4) is what makes the resolution granting it privileged in the first place.

Who sits on the committee's majority side is set by party rule rather than by House rule. Writing in December 2024, CRS recorded the practice as it stood in the 118th Congress: "Rule 12(b) of the GOP Conference of the 118th Congress (2023-2024) allows the Speaker to nominate the Republican members of the Rules Committee, subject to approval by the full conference. Democrats use the same appointment procedure when serving in the majority." That footnote names a specific conference rule of a specific Congress, and no source stored here establishes what the party rule says in the 119th.

How can a bill reach the floor without the Rules Committee?

Several routes exist, and Rule XV carries most of them.

Suspension of the rules. Under clause 1 of Rule XV, "A rule may not be suspended except by a vote of two-thirds of the Members voting, a quorum being present", and the Speaker may not entertain such a motion "except on Mondays, Tuesdays, and Wednesdays." The trade for that speed is debate: "A motion that the House suspend the rules is debatable for 40 minutes, one-half in favor of the motion and one-half in opposition thereto", and while such a motion is pending "the Speaker may entertain one motion that the House adjourn but may not entertain any other motion until the vote is taken on the suspension." CRS notes the House "may choose to consider legislation under suspension of the rules or by unanimous consent, but on major legislation a special rule is generally preferred because of the procedural flexibility it affords."

Inherent privilege. Some matters may be reported at any time under clause 5(a) of Rule XIII without a rule at all, including "general appropriation bills and on joint resolutions continuing appropriations for a fiscal year after September 15 in the preceding fiscal year" from the Appropriations Committee.

Calendar Wednesday. Clause 6 of Rule XV provides that "On Wednesday of each week, business shall not be in order before completion of the call of those committees (except as provided by clause 4 of rule XIV) whose chair, or other member authorized by the committee, has announced to the House a request for such call at least 72 hours in advance." A bill on the House or Union Calendar may be called up this way, "except bills or resolutions that are privileged under the Rules of the House" — the Manual notes a bill "privileged by reason of the Rules of the House cannot be called up on Calendar Wednesday". Debate is capped: "General debate on a measure considered under this clause shall be confined to the measure and may not exceed two hours equally divided between a proponent and an opponent." The clause also has a seasonal limit — it "does not apply during the last two weeks of a session of Congress."

The Consensus Calendar. Added in the 116th Congress, clause 7 of Rule XV requires that "At least once during any week in which the House convenes, the House shall consider a measure on the Consensus Calendar as designated by the Speaker", subject to a window that "does not apply before March 1 of an odd-numbered year or after September 30 of an even-numbered year." The sponsor of a measure "that has accumulated 290 cosponsors and has not been reported by the committee of primary jurisdiction" may move to place it there, and after the measure "has maintained at least 290 cosponsors for a cumulative period of 25 legislative days" it is placed on the calendar. The route closes as soon as the committee acts: a motion "shall be considered as withdrawn if the measure is reported by the committee of primary jurisdiction prior to its placement on the Consensus Calendar", and a measure already on the calendar stays there only "until it is-- (1) considered in the House; or (2) reported by the committee of primary jurisdiction."

The Private Calendar. For private bills and resolutions, clause 5 of Rule XV provides that "On the first Tuesday of a month, the Speaker shall direct the Clerk to call the bills and resolutions on the Private Calendar after disposal of such business on the Speaker's table as requires reference only." This route reaches only private legislation, not general public measures, and any two objectors end it: "If two or more Members, Delegates, or the Resident Commissioner object to the consideration of a bill or resolution so called, it shall be recommitted to the committee that reported it."

The discharge petition. This one reaches the Rules Committee itself. Clause 2 of Rule XV lets a member "present to the Clerk a motion in writing to discharge" either "a committee from consideration of a public bill or public resolution that has been referred to it for 30 legislative days", or "the Committee on Rules from consideration of a resolution that has been referred to it for seven legislative days and that proposes a special order of business for the consideration of a public bill or public resolution that has been reported by a committee or has been referred to a committee for 30 legislative days." The threshold in both cases is a majority of the total membership of the House — a phrase the Manual records as having been "construed to mean 218 Members (Speaker Byrns, Apr. 15, 1936, p. 5509)". The rule adds limits. "Only one motion may be presented for a bill or resolution", and "A Member may not file a motion to discharge the Committee on Rules from consideration of a resolution providing for the consideration of more than one public bill or public resolution or admitting or effecting a nongermane amendment to a public bill or public resolution." Elapsed time alone does not make a completed motion privileged: one that has been on the calendar "for at least seven legislative days (except during the last six days of a session of Congress)" is privileged "only at a time or place, designated by the Speaker, in the legislative schedule within two legislative days after the day on which a Member whose signature appears thereon announces to the House an intention to offer the motion." And once the House has acted on one, a further motion on substantially the same subject is out of order for the rest of the session. The discharge petition has its own page.

One route that no longer exists is the Consent Calendar. The 119th Congress House Manual carries it only as history: "Sec. 899. Former Consent Calendar. For the former Consent Calendar rule, see Sec. 899 of the House Rules and Manual for the 111th Congress (H. Doc. 110-162)."

What limits does the Rules Committee face?

Clause 6 of Rule XIII is where most of them sit. Under clause 6(a), a report from the committee "may not be called up for consideration on the same day it is presented to the House" except in four cases: "when so determined by a vote of two-thirds of the Members voting, a quorum being present"; "a resolution proposing only to waive a requirement of clause 4 or of clause 8 of rule XXII concerning the availability of reports"; text "made available to Members, Delegates, and the Resident Commissioner prior to the convening of that legislative day"; or "during the last three days of a session of Congress."

Under clause 6(c), the committee "may not report a rule or order that would prevent the motion to recommit a bill or joint resolution from being made as provided in clause 2(b) of rule XIX, if offered by the Minority Leader or a designee," with an exception for a Senate measure carrying substituted House-passed text.

Other paragraphs of the same clause bind it further. Clause 6(b) provides that while a Rules Committee report is pending, "the Speaker may entertain one motion that the House adjourn but may not entertain any other dilatory motion until the report shall have been disposed of." Clause 6(d) requires that the committee "present to the House reports concerning rules, joint rules, and the order of business, within three legislative days of the time when they are ordered", and provides that if such a report on the calendar "is not called up by the member of the committee who filed the report within seven legislative days thereafter, any member of the committee may call it up as a privileged question on the day after the calendar day on which the member announces to the House intention to do so. The Speaker shall recognize a member of the committee who seeks recognition for that purpose." Clause 6(e) makes an adverse report by the committee on a special-order resolution callable "as a privileged question by a Member, Delegate, or Resident Commissioner on the second and fourth Mondays of a month." And clause 6(g) requires that whenever the committee reports a resolution providing for consideration of a measure, "it shall to the maximum extent possible specify in the accompanying report any waiver of a point of order against the measure or against its consideration."

Rules Committee resolutions are, however, exempt from the House's ordinary 72-hour report-availability requirement. Clause 4(a)(1) of Rule XIII bars consideration of a reported measure until its report has been available "for 72 hours", and clause 4(a)(2)(A) excludes from that bar "a resolution providing a rule, joint rule, or order of business reported by the Committee on Rules considered under clause 6".

Access to the committee is open in one respect. CRS reports: "Any Member may testify before the Rules Committee prior to a special rule being issued". It points to a companion report whose topics "include how Members may submit amendments to the Rules Committee for possible inclusion under the rule and testify before the committee, the markup of the rule by the Rules Committee" and more.

Does the Senate have an equivalent?

No. The Senate arranges its floor business by unanimous consent rather than by an order-of-business resolution reported from a committee. CRS, in a report last updated on May 19, 2008, describes the chamber as fundamentally a unanimous consent institution, meaning "the rules and precedents of the Senate are set aside regularly by the unanimous consent of the membership." A House special rule is adopted by majority vote; the Senate's arrangements rest on unanimous consent instead.

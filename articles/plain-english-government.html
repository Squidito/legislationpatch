Why Plain-English Government Transparency Matters

By James Shearn, Editor Published May 2026Updated September 2026

The United States has a transparency problem that has nothing to do with classified information. Federal legislation — the laws that govern taxation, healthcare, surveillance, immigration, and virtually every other aspect of American life — is publicly available. Anyone can download the full text of any bill or resolution from 1993 forward, in PDF or plain text, and in XML since 2007. The documents exist, and they are online.

Reading them is another matter. And that's a problem with the documents, not the readers.

What Federal Bill Text Actually Looks Like

Here is a representative sentence from the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2026, signed into law on February 3, 2026 — the entire operative text of Section 5005:

"Section 225(e) of the Department of Homeland Security Appropriations Act, 2019 (division A of Public Law 116-6; 49 U.S.C. 44901 note) is amended by striking '2025' and inserting '2026'."

That sentence extends a Department of Homeland Security screening program by one year. One number, from 2025 to 2026. It's 29 words of cross-references to accomplish what could be stated in eight: "The program is extended from 2025 to 2026."

This is not exceptional. It is how the two sections either side of it read. Section 5004, immediately before, amends "Section 1309(a) of the National Flood Insurance Act of 1968 (42 U.S.C. 4016(a))". Section 5006, immediately after, applies a date substitution to "Section 4144(d) of the Motor Carrier Safety Reauthorization Act of 2005 (49 U.S.C. 31100 note; Public Law 109-59)". Each names what it amends, exactly: which statute, which section, which subsection.

The result is that the meaning of the sentence is not in the sentence. It is in Section 225(e) of the Act the sentence names, which the sentence does not reproduce.

The Gap Between Availability and Accessibility

Making information available is one standard of transparency in government. It is too low a standard: information that is technically available but functionally inaccessible leaves a reader in the same position as information that is not available at all.

Congress.gov carries bill and resolution text from 1993 forward, committee reports from 1995, and the full text of Senate submitted amendments from the 114th Congress onward. The Congressional Record's daily edition reports the previous day's business on days either chamber met, and is usually available by 10:00 a.m. Current-Congress information is usually updated the morning after House or Senate chamber sessions adjourn. All of that is on one website, on that schedule.

But making raw government data available isn't the same as making it accessible. Accessibility requires translation — not of language, but of form. The enrolled text of a bill needs to be readable by someone who doesn't spend their workday with the U.S. Code. The vote record needs to be displayed in a way that answers the question "how did my representative vote" without requiring the user to download a XML file and parse it.

Why This Matters Politically

The practical effect of inaccessible legislation is that a reader who cannot read the bill has to rely on someone's account of it instead. That is not an inherently bad position to be in. It is a limited one, and the limits are structural.

Any account shorter than the bill is selective. The Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2026 runs to nine divisions, and the one-sentence screening extension quoted above is a single section inside Division I, Authorizing Extenders and Technical Corrections. A provision like that one is law whether or not anyone writes about it.

Transparency that works only when someone else finds the provision first is incomplete transparency.

The Plain Language Movement

Plain language in government isn't a new idea. The Plain Writing Act of 2010 requires each agency to "use plain writing in every covered document of the agency that the agency issues or substantially revises." A covered document is "any document" that is "necessary for obtaining any Federal Government benefit or service or filing taxes", or that "provides information about any Federal Government benefit or service", or that "explains to the public how to comply with a requirement the Federal Government administers or enforces". The Act adds that a covered document "includes (whether in paper or electronic form) a letter, publication, form, notice, or instruction", and that it "does not include a regulation." Twelve years earlier the Securities and Exchange Commission had adopted a rule requiring issuers "to write the cover page, summary, and risk factors section of prospectuses in plain English", and announced alongside it "a final version of A Plain English Handbook: How to Create Clear SEC Disclosure Documents."

None of that reaches Congress. The Plain Writing Act defines "agency" as "an Executive agency, as defined under section 105 of title 5, United States Code", and section 105 in turn defines an Executive agency as "an Executive department, a Government corporation, and an independent establishment." The drafting of bills is not on that list. The Section 5005 sentence could not have said "change the expiration date" and worked: it has to name the statute, the subsection, the public law, the U.S. Code note, and both dates.

The answer isn't to change how bills are drafted. It's to build translation infrastructure on top of the existing system — tools that take the officially published text, read it carefully, and explain it accurately in language that doesn't require a legal background to understand.

What "No Editorial Spin" Actually Means

There's a version of accessible government information that's actually less useful than the original: the version that buries a genuine change in ideological framing. A bill that, for claims already timely under it, bars defenses and doctrines otherwise available to defendants in civil litigation is a factual change. Whether that change is just, overdue, or dangerous is an interpretive question on which reasonable people disagree.

Plain-English government transparency requires separating those two things. The bill is the Holocaust Expropriated Art Recovery Act of 2025, Public Law 119-82. Stated plainly, the factual change is this. For a claim that is "otherwise timely under this Act", two families of objection "may not be applied with respect to the claim". First, all "defenses or substantive doctrines based on the passage of time", which the statute says includes laches, adverse possession, acquisitive prescription and usucapion. Second, all "non-merits discretionary bases for dismissal", which it says includes the act of state doctrine, international comity, forum non conveniens, prudential exhaustion "and similar doctrines unrelated to the merits". Both lists are open — the statute says "including", and closes neither one. The change reaches any claim "pending in any court on the date of enactment of this Act, including any civil claim or cause of action that is pending on appeal or for which the time to file an appeal has not expired", and any claim "filed on or after the date of enactment of this Act." The interpretive question — was this the right call? — belongs to the reader, not the explainer.

Notice how much of that restatement is condition, that both lists stay open, and that the quotation marks sit only around the statute's own words. Drop "otherwise timely" and the plain-English version says something the statute does not.

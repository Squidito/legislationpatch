What Is Unanimous Consent in Congress? How Routine Business Gets Done

By James Shearn, EditorPublished May 2026Updated September 2026

Unanimous consent is how the Senate sets its own rules aside. The Congressional Research Service opens its report on the practice this way: "The Senate is fundamentally a “unanimous consent” institution. This means that the rules and precedents of the Senate are set aside regularly by the unanimous consent of the membership. Party leaders and other Senators propound unanimous consent requests every day the Senate is in session. Without its tradition of unanimous consent, the Senate would find it harder to process its complex workload."

The mechanics are as simple as they sound. A senator asks; the chair asks whether there is objection; if none is heard, it is so ordered. Any senator can object, and the request fails.

Two Different Things Are Called UC

CRS draws a distinction the phrase hides: "It is worth distinguishing between simple unanimous consent requests and complex unanimous consent agreements."

Simple requests are housekeeping. They "cover such routine matters as dispensing with quorum calls, asking that certain staff aides have floor privileges during consideration of a proposition, waiving the reading of amendments, setting aside amendments, or inserting material in the Congressional Record."

Complex agreements are negotiated instruments. They "establish the procedural guidelines for considering measures or matters on the floor. They are proposed orally on the floor, often by the majority leader, and they often result from lengthy negotiations among numerous Senators."

What such an agreement can do is broad. They may "identify when a measure is to be brought to the floor, set time limitations for debate on all amendments and motions," and they may "structure the offering of amendments, waive points of order against the measure or amendments thereto, or impose a requirement that all amendments be relevant to the measure under consideration."

And it binds like a contract: "In sum, a complex unanimous consent agreement is akin to a negotiated “contract” among all Senators, and it can only be changed by another unanimous consent agreement."

How UC Relates to Cloture

CRS's own comparison is careful, and worth quoting rather than paraphrasing: complex agreements "are a form of “voluntary cloture,” and their use facilitates the management of the Senate’s agenda and workload."

The two are alternatives at the same gate. Getting a measure onto the floor at all is a choice among three routes — "a vote on, invoking cloture on, or unanimous consent to a motion to proceed to consideration of the measure." When consent is not available, what remains is the formal route, cloture under Rule XXII: "If unsuccessful in obtaining clearance, the motion to proceed is debatable and therefore may be filibustered."

How a Noncontroversial Bill Actually Passes

On bills that draw no opposition, the asking often follows the answer. "On many pieces of noncontroversial legislation, Senate leaders might use one of two informal processes called clearance and hotlining to determine the feasibility of expeditious or immediate consideration of a measure." CRS defines both: "Clearance generally implies a process for obtaining Senators’ consent to call up and pass legislation on the Senate Calendar," while "Hotline or hotlining generally implies seeking Senators’ immediate response to a proposed action, such as the outline of a possible unanimous consent agreement or a proposal to within a few minutes pass a bill or resolution by unanimous consent."

The point of the exercise: "Senators are notified of pending noncontroversial bills to determine if any Senator would object to proceeding to consider and then passing a specific measure by unanimous consent" — "with little or no debate, no motion or amendment unless it is sought as part of clearance, and, likely, no recorded votes."

On the floor it is a short exchange. A senator asks "unanimous consent that the Senate proceed to the immediate consideration of" the bill. The Record then reads: "There being no objection, the Senate proceeded to consider the bill." The senator asks next "that the bill be read a third time and passed, and the motion to reconsider be laid upon the table," and the chair answers: "Without objection, it is so ordered." That can all happen the day the bill is introduced.

UC Can Skip the Committee Stage Entirely

"Provisions of Senate Rule XIV and the practice of unanimous consent allow the Senate to bypass a measure’s referral to committee, whether that measure might be major or noncontroversial." A measure can go straight onto the calendar, sit at the desk, or be pulled back out of committee: "Unanimous consent may be used to truncate a committee’s consideration of a measure referred to it: a measure might be referred to a committee but then the committee by unanimous consent of the Senate is discharged from further consideration of the measure."

All three routes end in the same place: "The Senate regularly uses unanimous consent to consider and pass noncontroversial legislation that was placed directly on the calendar, that is at the desk (neither placed on the calendar nor referred to committee), or that has been discharged from committee."

Nominations: Recorded as Votes, Not as Consent

Nominations are one place where "no recorded vote" is easily mistaken for "unanimous consent." Congress.gov's record for the 119th Congress holds 2,077 nomination records; 1,622 of them carry a latest action beginning "Confirmed by the Senate" as of August 31, 2026.

How those confirmations happened is recorded, and it is not consent. Counting the latest-action text of all 1,622: "Confirmed by Voice Vote: 1034", "Confirmed by Yea-Nay Vote: 588", "Confirmed by Unanimous Consent: 0". A voice vote is a vote, and it is filed as a different thing: "No 119th Congress nomination record carries a latest action of confirmation by unanimous consent; every confirmation is recorded as a voice vote or a yea-nay vote."

The Power of a Single Objection

The whole structure rests on one fact: "When a unanimous consent request is proposed on the floor, any Senator may object to it. If objection is heard, the consent request does not take effect."

The hold is the standing form of that threat. "The Senate “hold” is an informal practice whereby Senators communicate to Senate leaders, often in the form of a letter, their policy views and scheduling preferences regarding measures and matters available for floor consideration." It has no rule behind it: "The Senate’s standing rules do not address this practice, and the party leaders are not bound by such requests," and "ultimately the decision to honor a hold request, and for how long, rests with the majority leader."

It works anyway, for a reason CRS states plainly: "More often than not, Senate leaders honor a hold request because not doing so could trigger a range of parliamentary responses from the holding Senator(s), such as a filibuster, that could expend significant amounts of scarce floor time." CRS states the whole of it: "Fundamentally, however, when a Senator places the hold, he or she is implicitly registering his or her intention to object to any unanimous consent request for consideration of the measure."

Holds are not all one thing. "Informational holds, for instance, request that the Senator be notified or consulted in advance of any floor action to be taken on a particular measure or matter, perhaps to allow the Senator to plan for floor debate or the offering of amendments." "Choke holds contain an explicit filibuster threat and are intended to kill or delay action on the target of the hold." "Blanket holds are leveled against an entire category of business, such as all nominations to a particular agency or department."

They are also no longer entirely private. The Senate has adopted two measures that "sought to eliminate the secrecy of holds," under which a hold — "formally referred to in the new rules as “notices of intent to object to proceeding”" — becomes public if certain criteria are met. The first was "Section 512 of the Honest Leadership and Open Government Act ( P.L. 110-81 )" in 2007; the second, a standing order adopted at the start of the 112th Congress, "provides two days of session during which Senators are expected to deliver their objection notices for publication."

A Bill This Happened To — on One Side Only

The Holocaust Expropriated Art Recovery Act of 2025 (S. 1884) shows the distinction in the record. This site's own corpus snapshot for the bill stores two different methods, one per chamber: "vote: Senate 2025-12-10 "On Passage" Passed (Unanimous Consent)" and "vote: House 2026-03-16 "On Passage" Passed (Voice Vote)". The Senate passed it by unanimous consent; the House passed it by voice vote, which is a different thing recorded in a different category.

UC in the House

The House has no Consent Calendar. Where the rule used to be, its Rules and Manual for the 119th Congress carries only a pointer: "Sec. 899. Former Consent Calendar. For the former Consent Calendar rule, see Sec. 899 of the House Rules and Manual for the 111th Congress (H. Doc. 110-162)." The Corrections Calendar went the same way: "Sec. 898. Former Corrections Calendar."

The House route for measures that draw no real opposition is suspension of the rules, under clause 1 of Rule XV: "A rule may not be suspended except by a vote of two-thirds of the Members voting, a quorum being present." That is a supermajority vote, not consent — it has its own explainer here.

Unanimous consent does appear in the House, largely as an adjustment to those procedures rather than a route of its own. The Manual records that "The motion to suspend the rules can be made on days other than suspension days by unanimous consent," and that the motion "may be withdrawn by unanimous consent, even after the Speaker has put the question on its adoption and postponed further proceedings."

What Is the Congressional Record? How to Use the Official Floor Transcript

By James Shearn, Editor Published May 2026Updated August 2026

The Congressional Record is the official record of the proceedings and debates of the United States Congress. Published by the Government Publishing Office on every day Congress is in session, it carries the debate on bills and resolutions, the rollcall and voice votes taken on them, and — in a section called Extensions of Remarks — material members submit for the record without necessarily speaking or reading it on the floor.

It began publication in 1873. GovInfo's daily Congressional Record collection covers volume 140 (1994) to the present, and the current year's database is usually updated by 11 a.m.; the Bound Congressional Record and the Record Index sit in separate collections there. Except for copyrighted articles, GovInfo places no restrictions on republishing material from the Record.

What the Congressional Record Contains

The Record is divided into four main sections:

House section. The public proceedings and debates of the U.S. House of Representatives — both words spoken on the floor and words inserted or appended by members. Its pages carry an H prefix (H1234). Statements are formatted with a speaker identifier in capitals (Mr. CORNYN, Ms. WARREN) followed by the member's words, which is what makes automated extraction possible.

Senate section. The public proceedings and debates of the U.S. Senate, on the same terms: spoken words and inserted or appended words together. Its pages carry an S prefix.

Extensions of Remarks. Tributes, statements, and other information that supplements statements made by members on the floor of either chamber, including the bulk of House remarks that were never delivered. Its pages carry an E prefix (E1234); the daily Record's sections are numbered separately and consecutively in each session, so the prefix alone tells you which section a citation points at.

Daily Digest. A table of contents for the day: highlights, then a summary for each chamber covering measures introduced, reported, passed or under consideration, plus the committee meetings held and scheduled. Its pages carry a D prefix.

Revise and Extend, and What It Actually Means

The Congressional Research Service describes the Record as a substantially verbatim account of remarks made during the proceedings of the House and Senate, subject only to technical, grammatical, and typographical corrections.

In the House, a member may ask the presiding officer for permission to revise and extend — which CRS glosses as expanding their statements. Senators and representatives may also have remarks and other material printed in the Record without ever speaking or reading the text on the floor.

Whether that undelivered material is marked depends on the chamber. In the House proceedings, any portion of a statement not spoken is printed in a different type style. In the Senate, a bullet symbol precedes and distinguishes undelivered remarks — but CRS also records that in the Senate, with unanimous consent, undelivered remarks are printed as if spoken. So appearing in the House or Senate proceedings does not by itself establish that something was said out loud, and Extensions of Remarks is where most, not all, of the undelivered House material appears.

How to Search the Record

On GovInfo you can run a keyword search over the full text, refine the results by the Record's own metadata, or retrieve a single document by citation when you already know the volume (year), the page type — House, Senate, Extensions or Daily Digest — and the page number.

To find what a member said about a specific bill, search for the bill's short title (e.g., "Holocaust Expropriated Art Recovery Act") filtered to the date range around the bill's floor vote. CRS calls the Daily Digest the key to using the daily Record: it is the last section of each issue and serves as an index, letting a reader locate the times of meetings of both houses, the measures reported, considered, or signed into law, and the committee activity for the previous and current days.

GovInfo breaks the Record into "granules" — individual documents within a day's issue — each with its own identifier, and publishes a predictable URL for one: govinfo.gov/app/details/{packageId}/{granuleId}. That makes it possible to address a specific floor statement rather than the entire day's Record.

Why the Congressional Record Matters for Understanding Legislation

When a statute becomes the subject of a dispute in court, judges usually must interpret the law, ambiguous or not — and one of the tools they may use is its legislative history, the record of Congress's deliberations when enacting it. Floor debate is part of that record.

It is not the strongest part of it. The Congressional Research Service's survey of statutory interpretation reports that committee reports provide stronger evidence of Congress's understanding of a law than individual floor statements from non-sponsors, and that floor statements — especially those made by a bill's sponsors before passage — may be relevant but should be used cautiously. In one case CRS cites, the Supreme Court put the limit plainly: even from a sponsor, a single outlying statement cannot stand against a tide of context and history.

How LegislationPatch Uses the Congressional Record

We keep member quotes in two places, and they carry different provenance. The standalone floor-quote collection behind our Floor Activity page is extracted granule by granule: 430 of its 468 quotes store the GovInfo granule identifier they came from.

The quotes on a bill's own page come from a second extractor with a wider net. Each excerpt is sliced straight out of the fetched text, so it is verbatim by construction rather than by a later check. It fetches the Record text for that bill's floor dates, and it also pulls the minority, additional and dissenting views out of the bill's committee reports — a different GovInfo collection, not the Record. Those quotes are stored with the speaker's name, party and state, and the chamber, but not with a granule identifier: none of the 417 carries one. Forty of our 219 bills carry no quote at all.

Every quote in either store is reproduced verbatim from the document it came from, never paraphrased, and capped at 550 characters trimmed back to a sentence end. Neither store carries a session field, and we do not claim one.

We use the Record for what members said on the floor about a bill: their explanation of what it does, their objections to it, and their statements about what it is meant to do.

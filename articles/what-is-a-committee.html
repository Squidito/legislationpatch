What Is a Congressional Committee — and Why Bills Die There

By James Shearn, Editor Published May 2026 Updated September 2026

Congress runs its work through committees, and the rules give a committee no obligation to act on what it receives. Congress.gov recorded 18,500 measures introduced in the 119th Congress as of September 1, 2026. Those measures are not comparable to one another: 2,505 of them are concurrent or simple resolutions, which cannot become law at all, and the 119th Congress is still in session, so no completion rate can be read off that figure.

The Congressional Research Service describes the system those measures enter: "Congress divides its legislative, oversight, and internal administrative tasks among more than 200 committees and subcommittees."

What is a congressional committee?

It is a panel with jurisdiction over a defined subject area and the power to decide what happens to measures referred to it. CRS lists what committees do within their assigned areas: they "gather information; compare and evaluate legislative alternatives; identify policy problems and propose solutions; select, determine, and report measures for full chamber consideration; monitor executive branch performance (oversight); and investigate allegations of wrongdoing."

The modern structure dates to a single statute: "The 1946 Legislative Reorganization Act (60 Stat. 812) sets the framework for the modern committee system", CRS writes, adding that the act "organized the Senate and House committees along roughly parallel lines, but divergences have emerged over time."

Writing in November 2025, CRS counted the 119th Congress as having "20 standing committees in the House, with 104 subcommittees" and "two select committees." On the Senate side it counted "16 standing committees, with 70 subcommittees" and, in the same sentence, "four select or special committees. There are also four joint committees." (CRS carries footnote markers inside those sentences, so each figure is quoted as its own fragment rather than as one continuous run.)

Types of Committees

CRS identifies "three main types of committees: standing, select or special, and joint", and separately describes conference committees and subcommittees.

Standing committees

Standing committees are "permanent panels identified as such in chamber rules (House Rule X, Senate Rule XXV)." House Rule X states it directly: "There shall be in the House the following standing committees, each of which shall have the jurisdiction and related functions assigned by this clause and clauses 2, 3, and 4."

Because they hold legislative jurisdiction, CRS says, standing committees "consider bills and issues and recommend measures for consideration by their respective chambers", and they "also have oversight responsibility to monitor agencies, programs, and activities within their jurisdictions". Most recommend authorization levels; a few have other roles. The Appropriations Committees "recommend legislation to provide budget authority for federal agencies and programs", and the Budget Committees "establish aggregate levels for total spending and revenue, via the annual budget resolution, that serve as guidelines for the work of the authorizing and appropriating panels."

House Rule X sets out each committee's jurisdiction as a numbered list. Four examples, quoted from the rule:

  • Appropriations — "Appropriation of the revenue for the support of the Government", "Rescissions of appropriations contained in appropriation Acts", and "Transfers of unexpended balances."
  • Ways and Means — "Revenue measures generally", "Reciprocal trade agreements", "Deposit of public monies", and "National social security" with carve-outs the rule states.
  • Armed Services — "Common defense generally", "The Department of Defense generally, including the Departments of the Army, Navy, and Air Force, generally", and "Military applications of nuclear energy."
  • Judiciary — "The judiciary and judicial proceedings, civil and criminal", "Constitutional amendments", "Bankruptcy, mutiny, espionage, and counterfeiting", and "Immigration policy and non-border enforcement."

Select and special committees

These are "generally established by a separate resolution of the chamber, sometimes to conduct investigations and studies and, on other occasions, also to consider measures", CRS writes. They often "examine emerging issues that do not fit clearly within existing standing committee jurisdictions or cut across jurisdictional boundaries", and one "may be permanent or temporary." They "may have certain restrictions on member tenure or may include certain specified representatives" as ex officio members, and the Senate "sometimes uses special committee (e.g., the Special Committee on Aging)" instead of the word select. CRS notes that one of the House's two select committees, the House Permanent Select Committee on Intelligence, "operates, in many ways, like a permanent standing committee".

Joint committees

Joint committees "are made up of Members of both the House and Senate", and today's permanent ones "conduct studies or perform housekeeping tasks rather than consider measures" — CRS gives the Joint Committee on Printing, which "oversees the functions of the Government Publishing Office and general printing procedures of the federal government." The chairmanship "usually alternates between the House and Senate."

Conference committees

A conference committee is "a temporary joint committee formed to resolve differences between competing House and Senate versions of a measure." Conference committees "draft compromises between the positions of the two chambers, which are then submitted to the full House and Senate for approval."

Subcommittees

Most committees create subcommittees, which "are responsible to their parent committees and work within the guidelines established by them." The rules differ by chamber: "Senate rules do not directly limit the number of subcommittees each committee may create", while "House rules impose a maximum of five subcommittees for most committees (Rule X, clause 5(d)), but a sixth oversight subcommittee is permitted", with larger allowances written in for some committees. CRS adds that practice varies: some committees "routinely refer measures to subcommittees for initial consideration", while "on other committees, most work is undertaken by the full committees."

How does a bill move through a committee?

Referral

In the House, referral is the Speaker's job and the rule is mandatory: "The Speaker shall refer each bill, resolution, or other matter that relates to a subject listed under a standing committee named in clause 1 of rule X in accordance with the provisions of this clause." Where more than one committee has a claim, the Speaker "shall designate a committee of primary jurisdiction" and "may refer the matter to one or more additional committees for consideration in sequence, either initially or after the matter has been reported by the committee of primary jurisdiction", or "may refer portions of the matter reflecting different subjects and jurisdictions" to different committees.

The Senate refers most measures too, but has a standing bypass. CRS reports that "most bills and joint resolutions introduced in the Senate, and many House-numbered bills and joint resolutions received by the Senate after House passage, are referred to committee" — while Senate Rule XIV, which "requires measures to be read twice before referral to committee", lets a senator object after the second reading, which "prevents a bill or joint resolution's referral to committee" and places the measure "directly on the Senate Calendar of Business."

Meetings, and who can call one

House Rule XI requires each standing committee to "establish regular meeting days for the conduct of its business, which shall be not less frequent than monthly." The chair "may call and convene, as the chair considers necessary, additional and special meetings of the committee for the consideration of a bill or resolution pending before the committee or for the conduct of other committee business".

The chair does not hold that power alone. Under the same clause, "Three or more members of a standing committee may file in the offices of the committee a written request that the chair call a special meeting of the committee", and "if the chair does not call the requested special meeting within three calendar days after the filing of the request (to be held within seven calendar days after the filing of the request) a majority of the members of the committee may file in the offices of the committee their written notice that a special meeting of the committee will be held." That notice must name the date, hour and measure, and "only the measure or matter specified in that notice may be considered at that special meeting."

Hearings and markup

Committee business is presumptively public, and the rule covers markups by name. Under Rule XI clause 2(g)(1), "Each meeting for the transaction of business, including the markup of legislation, by a standing committee or subcommittee thereof (other than the Committee on Ethics or its subcommittees) shall be open to the public, including to radio, television, and still photography coverage," unless the committee votes in open session, with a majority present, to close it "because disclosure of matters to be considered would endanger national security, would compromise sensitive law enforcement information, would tend to defame, degrade, or incriminate any person, or otherwise would violate a law or rule of the House."

Hearings sit under the next subparagraph and carry a shorter list. Clause 2(g)(2)(A) opens hearings to the public on the same terms, with the same Ethics carve-out, but permits closure only where disclosure "would endanger national security, would compromise sensitive law enforcement information, or would violate a law or rule of the House" — the defamation ground that applies to business meetings is absent.

Reporting and the committee report

Reporting is a duty once the committee has acted: "It shall be the duty of the chair of each committee to report or cause to be reported promptly to the House a measure or matter approved by the committee and to take or cause to be taken steps necessary to bring the measure or matter to a vote." And if the chair does not, "the report of a committee on a measure that has been approved by the committee shall be filed within seven calendar days (exclusive of days on which the House is not in session) after the day on which a written request for the filing of the report, signed by a majority of the members of the committee, has been filed with the clerk of the committee."

The report itself has required contents. Rule XIII clause 3(b) provides that "with respect to each record vote on a motion to report a measure or matter of a public nature, and on any amendment offered to the measure or matter, the total number of votes cast for and against, and the names of members voting for and against, shall be included in the committee report." One committee is carved out: "The preceding sentence does not apply to votes taken in executive session by the Committee on Ethics." Clause 3(c) requires the report to set out separately, among other items, "oversight findings and recommendations under clause 2(b)(1) of rule X" and "an estimate and comparison prepared by the Director of the Congressional Budget Office under section 402 of the Congressional Budget Act of 1974 if timely submitted to the committee before the filing of the report." Dissenting and supplemental views filed under Rule XI clause 2(l) "shall be included in, and shall be a part of, the report filed by the committee with respect to a measure or matter."

Why do so many bills die in committee?

Because nothing in the rules requires a committee to act on a measure referred to it. The duties above attach to a measure the committee has already approved; the rules impose no corresponding duty to take one up. What they supply instead is a route around inaction, and it is built to be hard to use.

That route is the discharge petition. Under clause 2 of House Rule XV, a member may move to discharge "a committee from consideration of a public bill or public resolution that has been referred to it for 30 legislative days", and the motion becomes effective only when signed by a majority of the total membership of the House — a phrase the Manual records as "construed to mean 218 Members (Speaker Byrns, Apr. 15, 1936, p. 5509)". The House Rules Committee holds the corresponding gate on floor scheduling, under a separate rule.
